# Stock-Bond Correlation and Treasury Term Premia

For several years the relationship between stocks and bonds has played a central role in how investors think about risk and the pricing of Treasuries. When stocks fall and bonds improve Treasuries provide protection, so when the two move together, that protection weakens, however this stock-bond relationship doesn’t necessarily explain how Treasuries are priced in 2025.

To explore this, I ran a pilot analysis focusing on the 10 year Treasury term premium (ACMTP10) rather than the more abstract concept of convenience yield. While the two are not interchangeable, both represent non expected rate components of Treasury yields, meaning they look at or measure forces beyond the path of short term interest rates. Term premia compensate investors for duration and inflation risk, while the convenience yield reflects non monetary benefits like liquidity and hedging value. Since both measures tend to move with risk conditions, the term premia provides a clean and easier way to test whether traditional relationships are breaking down. It is easier to measure than the convenience yield because it can be explicitly measured unlike the convenience yield which doesn’t have a quantitative value.

Using SPY returns and long maturity Treasury returns over the past five years, I constructed a rolling stock bond correlation, focusing primarily on a 60 day window to balance responsiveness and to reduce extra noise. I then estimated the historical relationship between this rolling correlation and the term premium using data prior to 2025. This served as a baseline for what the term premium should look like given past stock-bond dynamics.

The key test came next. Using the pre 2025 relationship, I generated out of sample predictions for 2025 and compared them to actual term premia. The results showed persistent deviations between predicted and actual values. To assess whether these differences were meaningful, I examined standardized residuals and flagged periods exceeding plus or minus 2 standard deviations relative to historical volatility. Several post 2025 deviations crossed this threshold, which suggests that recent behavior cannot be dismissed as normal noise.

While this analysis focused on term premia, the broader implication is about Treasury pricing more generally. If stock-bond correlation no longer explains risk compensation in the way it once did, it sparks doubt in the value of Treasuries for reasons that extend beyond traditional diversification and duration risk. At minimum, this pilot suggests that something has shifted. Whether that shift reflects temporary movement due to political uncertainty or a more structural change in how Treasuries are priced is the question that comes next.

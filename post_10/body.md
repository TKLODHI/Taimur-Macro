# The Tariff Whiplash Economy, Part Two: The Clock Runs Out

*Section 122 dies by statute on July 24. What replaces it was basically decided in a room full of trade lawyers you've never heard of.*

Five months ago the Supreme Court ended one tariff regime. On July 24, a second one, its stopgap replacement, expires automatically, whether or not anyone is ready for what comes next.

## How we got to a 150-day countdown

After Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump struck down the IEEPA tariffs in February, the administration didn't wait around. Within days, President Trump invoked Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 to impose a 10% surcharge on virtually all imports, effective February 24, and later raised it to the statutory maximum of 15%. The catch, and it's a big one, is that Section 122 was never built to be a durable policy tool. Congress capped it at 150 days from the effective date, and only Congress, not the president, can extend it beyond that. That clock runs out at 12:01 a.m. EDT on July 24, 2026.

Unlike IEEPA, this wasn't really a gray area. Section 122, unlike IEEPA, explicitly contemplates tariff authority in the statute itself, Congress wrote the emergency-surcharge power into the law on purpose; they just also wrote in an expiration date. Which is why nobody in the trade bar treats July 24 as a soft deadline. It's a hard sunset, full stop.

## Congress isn't picking up the phone

So will Congress extend it? All signs point to no. If anything, the legislative energy is moving the opposite direction, lawmakers have introduced the "Reclaim Trade Powers Act," aimed at constraining presidential tariff authority rather than renewing it. There's no urgency on the Hill to save a tariff regime that was always sold as temporary, and with midterm season approaching, voting to extend a tax that's added meaningfully to household costs isn't exactly a layup for either party.

## The administration's plan B (and C)

The White House isn't waiting on Congress either. It's leaning on Section 301, the same statute historically used for the China tariffs, as the durable successor. On June 2, USTR concluded investigations into the forced-labor enforcement policies of 60 major trading partners and proposed new tariffs ranging from 10% to 12.5% on imports from those countries. The split: a 10% rate for 15 trading partners that have negotiated arrangements with the administration, and 12.5% for the remaining 45, a group that includes major economies still finalizing terms. Practically, that means countries like Brazil, China, India, Japan, and South Korea landing in the higher 12.5% bracket once the new regime takes effect.

There's a real timing gap to watch here. USTR's own completion deadline on these Section 301 determinations lands July 20, four days before Section 122 dies. If the paperwork slips, importers could face a brief window where the surcharge lapses and duties temporarily fall back toward pre-IEEPA levels: MFN rates plus whatever Section 232 and legacy China-specific duties are already on the books. Nobody serious expects that gap to last, but a few days of lower landed costs would be a strange, almost comic coda to a year defined by tariffs going one direction only.

And Section 301 isn't the only tool in play. USTR has also opened Section 301 investigations into structural excess manufacturing capacity across 16 of the largest trading partners, which gives the administration a second lever to pull if the forced-labor track stalls or gets challenged in court — and it will get challenged. Importers affected by any final Section 301 action can challenge the tariffs before the Court of International Trade, though Section 301 has historically drawn far less judicial scrutiny than IEEPA did. That's the quiet reason the administration picked this route: it's not that Section 301 is faster, it's that it's harder to get struck down.

## Why the legal clothing matters more than the rate

It's tempting to read this as a wash, 15% becomes roughly 12.5%, a modest step down. But the more important story is durability, not the headline number. IEEPA tariffs got killed by the Supreme Court because the statute was never meant to function as a general taxing power. Section 122 was always going to expire because Congress built in a timer. Section 301, by contrast, is the tool Washington has used for decades to run long, sticky trade actions, it's reviewed every four years rather than every five months, and it survives changes in administration far more easily. If the July shift sticks, the "temporary" tariff era gives way to something closer to permanent trade policy, just dressed up as a series of country-specific enforcement actions instead of one sweeping emergency order.

## What actually gets more expensive, and for whom

The stacking matters as much as the headline rate. A Chinese-origin good that faced enormous IEEPA-era duties now sits under a combined Section 301-plus-122 rate in the low-to-mid 30s percent, and whatever comes after July 24 layers on top of existing China-specific Section 301 tariffs that predate this whole saga, not instead of them. Meanwhile, the EU carved out better terms months ago and is largely insulated from the reset, which tells you something about how unevenly this new regime is going to land. The countries without a negotiated deal, several large Asian manufacturing hubs among them, are the ones absorbing the 12.5% bracket by default, not because they negotiated it, but because they didn't negotiate anything at all.

## What to watch next

July 20 is the real date to circle, not July 24, that's when USTR's own deadline forces its hand on the Section 301 determinations, four days ahead of the Section 122 sunset. Watch whether the Federal Circuit appeal on Section 122's legality (running on a similar track to the IEEPA refund fight that returned $166 billion to importers) produces any surprises before then. And watch Congress's midterm-season appetite for touching tariffs at all, my guess is they'd rather let the executive branch own this one, legal risk included, than cast a vote either extending or repealing a tax that's already unpopular with households.

The throughline from the last entry still holds: the Supreme Court's ruling was a genuine constitutional check, but it was never going to function as an economic reset button. Five months later, the tariff whiplash economy hasn't ended, it's just changed its legal address again.

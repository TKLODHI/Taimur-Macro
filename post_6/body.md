# What’s Happening to the Convenience Yield?

In the past year, investors have noticed unusual behavior in US Treasury yields, raising doubts about their long-held status as a safe-haven asset. Historically, US Treasuries have been considered reliable during periods of economic or geopolitical uncertainty because they retain value when risk rises. However, this perception has been challenged recently due to rising inflation, higher interest rates, and growing concerns about fiscal deficits, which have increased perceived risk and diminished their image as a risk-free investment.

A key factor in understanding this phenomenon is the convenience yield—the premium investors place on U.S. Treasury securities due to their safety and liquidity relative to private assets. Traditionally, a strong convenience yield reflects the value of Treasuries as a hedge against uncertainty. Yet, there has been limited exploration of how the diminished safe-haven status of Treasuries is reflected in this yield and its relationship with the correlation between stocks and bonds.

The relationship between convenience yield and stock–bond covariance is well established as an inverse one. When stock–bond covariance is low or negative, Treasuries serve as an effective hedge against equity risk, boosting their value to investors and increasing the convenience yield. Conversely, when stock–bond covariance is positive, Treasuries lose much of their hedging appeal, leading to a decline in the convenience yield. While this relationship is known historically, research examining whether the convenience yield underperforms due to Treasuries’ reduced safe-haven status is still emerging.

Recent analyses suggest that Treasury convenience yields primarily reflect their hedging value, with stock–bond correlation being a significant driver. A decomposition of movements in the convenience yield shows that shifts in stock–bond correlation have a direct impact on its value.

Despite recognition of the diminished safe-haven status of Treasuries and the role of the convenience yield, there is still a gap in understanding how the two are linked. In particular, little has been said about the fair valuation of the convenience yield relative to stock–bond correlation—that is, what level of convenience yield would be expected given historical patterns, and how does it compare to current observations? Comparing observed convenience yields to these model-implied levels can provide insight into whether the perceived safe-haven status of Treasuries has truly declined.

# A Depletion in Turkey’s Foreign Reserves

Turkey's foreign reserves have taken a significant hit since the outbreak of war between Israel and Iran in late February. According to calculations by Bürümcekçi Research and Consulting based on official data, the central bank sold roughly $26bn of foreign currency in the three weeks leading up to March 19 alone, dragging net reserves (excluding swaps) down to just $43.4bn. Other independent economists put the damage even higher, estimating a drop of around $34bn in net reserves since the war began. The scale of the intervention underscores how directly exposed Turkey is to a conflict playing out on its doorstep, a point Finance Minister Mehmet Şimşek acknowledged himself, admitting it was "impossible" for Turkey to remain unaffected by the war.

Turkey shares a 550km border with Iran and is trying to mediate an end to the war, and is particularly exposed to the conflict as it imports most of its energy needs. Since the war started, Brent has risen by more than $30 a barrel, hovering at around $102; that's a big added import bill for an energy-dependent economy. Additionally, with a war next door, foreign investors who'd parked money in Turkey, which are attractive to investors due to their high interest rates, are pulling out of those assets. This then pressures the lira and weakens the currency. In order to defend the lira and keep the currency stable, the central bank has resorted to selling dollars.

Another cause that has provoked this drain in foreign reserves is the widening current account deficit. The finance minister flagged this as his top worry, and indeed Turkey's annualised current account deficit rose to almost $33bn. Higher energy import costs are the main driver of that widening gap, and a bigger deficit itself is a currency vulnerability.

Lastly, this reserve depletion feeds into a self-reinforcing cycle with inflation. Turkey imports most of its energy, so a weaker lira directly raises the cost of everything it brings in from abroad, energy above all, given Brent has risen more than $30 a barrel since the war began. Each dollar the central bank spends defending the currency is meant to prevent exactly this kind of imported inflation, but reserves are finite, and economists are already questioning how long Turkey can keep this up. If reserves run too low and the bank is forced to let the lira slide, the pass-through to prices would likely be sharp and immediate, which is part of why inflation already ticked up to 31.5 per cent last month, one of the highest rates in the world. That uptick then undercuts three years of disinflation progress and erodes the confidence that's kept foreign investors in Turkish assets in the first place, the very capital whose outflow started this cycle.

The sharp drop in Turkey's foreign reserves since the war began is sparking inquiries into whether the central bank will have to turn to its gold holdings to prop up the currency. The bank holds more than $100bn in gold, according to JPMorgan estimates, of which roughly $30bn is held at the Bank of England, reserves that could reportedly be deployed to intervene in currency markets without the logistical constraints that come with repatriating physical gold. Bloomberg reported this week that the central bank was already weighing gold swaps as a tool to bolster its foreign reserves, a mechanism that would involve temporarily exchanging gold for foreign currency, with an agreement to reverse the transaction at a later date. It's a way of unlocking liquidity without technically selling off the country's gold holdings outright. For fund managers watching the situation, the maths is becoming increasingly hard to ignore. "The central bank's current foreign exchange policy can't last much longer at this pace of reserves loss, unless they sell some of their gold reserves," said Kieran Curtis, an emerging markets fund manager at Aberdeen. His comment cuts to the core tension facing Ankara: the central bank has so far relied on spending down its dollar reserves to defend the lira, but with net reserves already down to $43.4bn and falling by the week, gold may soon become less of a backup option and more of a necessity.

Turkey has been here before, and in an even worse predicament. Last March, defending the lira after the arrest of Istanbul mayor Ekrem İmamoğlu cost the central bank as much as $50bn, and reserves fell to just $10bn before being rebuilt. "Turkey has held out well and performed better than many expected," said Timothy Ash, senior sovereign strategist at RBC BlueBay Asset Management. But he warned that patience has limits: the longer the war goes on and energy prices stay elevated, "almost everyone agrees what would have to happen: Turkey would have to let the lira go and raise interest rates." As Ash put it, in that scenario, "the whole world would be suffering," a reminder that Turkey's reserves crunch, however localized it may look now, is really just one visible symptom of a much larger regional shock.

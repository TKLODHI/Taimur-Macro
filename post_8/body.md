# Venezuela’s Debt Problem

Venezuela’s web of liabilities is amongst the most complex found across emerging markets. Years of default, sanctions, expropriations, and political instability have built this web of claims from hedge funds, governments and companies. As the country moves closer to a potential restructuring following the capturing of Maduro, prospective creditors are faced with three categories of instruments: sovereign debt, corporate debt, and expropriation claims. Each comes with their own unique risks, but also opportunities.

## Sovereign Debt

Out of Venezuela's outstanding debt, sovereign bonds make up the largest share of these liabilities. Venezuela's government and the state oil company, PDVSA, owe more than $100 billion – with unpaid interest included – to bondholders. The original debt principal was far smaller than this current debt, however since Venezuela hasn’t serviced these bonds since 2017, the interest has accumulated rapidly, which has made recovery prospects quite sensitive to the country’s economic rebound and oil production. Sovereign debt restructurings are often negotiated by collective agreements, or in other words most bondholders must accept similar terms. However in the case of Venezuela, this process of debt restructuring is not so straightforward due to a few factors: U.S. sanctions, uncertainty over political power, and unclear bilateral debts – especially to China. Up to 2017, China’s Development Bank (CDB) had lent billions of dollars to Venezuela, with the repayment coming out of oil sales. The remaining loans from China likely are highly distressed. Venezuela had to offer discounts on its oil to Chinese buyers, even before the US embargo. Despite this however these bonds are liquid and are likely to be integral to any possible restructuring.

## Corporate Debt

Corporate debt, specifically the debt from the PDVSA and their subsidiaries, has a middle ground between the sovereign bonds and the legal claims. The state oil company’s bonds and obligations are considered corporate, however they are very closely connected to the government of Venezuela, so in a sense they are heavily tied to the sovereign debts. As a result many investors view this PDVSA debt as a kind of proxy for sovereign risk, since the company’s fortune depends almost entirely on the state policy and oil revenues. A key difference between the sovereign bondholders and the corporate debt holders is that the latter may have stronger legal claims, especially where assets such as Citgo are involved. However, court supervised sales and competing creditor claims have made outcomes quite unpredictable. While corporate debt can offer more targeted opportunities for recovery, it is still entangled with the broader restructuring process of the country, and the oil sector.

## Expropriation Claims

Expropriation claims have their origin in Hugo Chavez’ mass nationalization in 2006—particularly of assets belonging to oil majors operating in the country. Companies like ConocoPhillips and ExxonMobil have won large arbitration awards through the international courts, a lot of these were then sold to hedge funds. More than $20 billion of claims came from companies that had their assets expropriated by the state of Venezuela. These claims are not bonds, and also they don’t require creditor coordination, which gives holders greater independence. So while these bond prices have risen since the kidnapping of Maduro, hedge funds have shot to these expropriation claims because they could potentially offer leverage over specific assets and do not need to be held to bondholder voting rules. However the enforcement remains tricky and politically sensitive. While these claims can be lucrative they also are the most uncertain and complex legally when it comes to recovery of said claims.

## The “better” Investment

Considering the three debts, each one offers different risks and rewards and the considered “better” investment really depends on how much risk an investor is willing to withstand. The sovereign bonds are not only the most straightforward claims, but also the most liquid. They will also almost certainly sit at the center of restructuring, however that being said it also makes them vulnerable to larger haircuts and also political pressure to prioritize economic recovery over creditor payouts. The corporate debt, particularly the debt linked to the state oil company, offers potential access to specific assets and stronger legal positioning in some jurisdictions, however it is important to acknowledge that it is still quite entangled with sovereign politics, especially with the U.S.’s interference in Venezuela, and it can become crowded by competing claims which would suggest that there is less certainty of recovery. Lastly the expropriation claims stand apart from the first two because they are backed by international arbitration awards and are not a part of bondholder votes, which gives holders greater independence in demanding repayment. However, enforcement of such claims are slow, and dependent on court rulings rather than negotiated settlements. In terms of investment, the expropriation claims appear to offer the greatest advantage for investors willing to accept the long timelines and also legal intricacy, on the other hand, sovereign bonds are advantageous for those seeking broader exposure to Venezuela's recovery with lower execution risk. So in conclusion, sovereign debt offers restructuring certainty, corporate debt offers more targeted asset exposure, and a big advantage in owning arbitration claims is that they are not subject to bondholder votes in a restructuring, making the latter the most attractive option for risk tolerant investors betting on a drawn out recovery.
